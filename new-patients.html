New patients

Welcome. We're glad you're here.

Starting mental health care can feel like a big step. This guide explains what happens from your first call to your first session, what to bring, and how your privacy is protected, so you know what to expect.

Before you begin

You don't have to have it all figured out

Many people reach out without knowing exactly what they need, only that things feel harder than they should. That's a perfectly good reason to start. Our care coordinators will listen, answer your questions and help you find the right clinician and type of care.

There's no wrong way to ask for help, and nothing you share in a first conversation commits you to anything.

Getting started

From first contact to first session

Four simple steps. We'll guide you through each one.

  1. Reach out

    Request an appointment online or call us at (555) 010-0123. A care coordinator will get back to you within one business day.

  2. Free phone consult

    A short, friendly call about what's bringing you in, your scheduling needs and preferences, so we can match you with the right clinician.

  3. Insurance & paperwork

    We'll check your benefits and send intake forms through our secure patient portal. Most people finish them in about 20 minutes.

  4. Your first appointment

    Meet your clinician in person or by video. You'll talk about your goals and start building a plan that fits you.

Your first appointment

What to expect

Your first visit is called an intake. It usually lasts 50–60 minutes (up to 90 for a psychiatric evaluation) and is mostly a conversation.

Getting to know you

Your clinician will ask what's bringing you in, how long things have felt this way, and what you'd like to be different.

Questions we may ask

About your mood, sleep, stress, relationships, work or school, health, medications, substance use, and any past care. These help us understand the full picture.

It's OK not to know

"I'm not sure" is a completely fine answer. You don't need a diagnosis or the right words. Figuring it out is our job, together.

You can bring someone

A partner, family member or friend is welcome to join for part or all of the visit if that helps you feel more comfortable. Just let us know.

You set the pace

You never have to share more than you're ready to. You can skip a question, take a break, or come back to something later.

Leaving with a plan

By the end, you'll talk through initial goals, a suggested type of care and how often to meet. Your questions are welcome throughout.

Prepare

What to bring

A little preparation helps your first visit run smoothly, but don't worry if you can't gather everything. We can follow up later.

  • Photo ID, such as a driver's license or state ID
  • Insurance card (front and back), or let us know if you're paying yourself
  • A list of current medications, including doses, supplements and over-the-counter medicines
  • Prior records if you have them: past evaluations, hospital discharge papers or notes from previous providers (we can also request these with your permission)
  • Your questions, written down if that helps. Nothing is too small to ask
  • Names of other providers you'd like us to coordinate with, such as your primary care doctor

Forms you can finish from home

Telehealth

Getting ready for a video visit

Video visits are just as effective for most people, and you get the same clinicians. A few minutes of prep makes them easier.

How telehealth works
  • Use a phone, tablet or computer with a camera, microphone and a steady internet connection
  • Find a private, quiet spot where you won't be overheard. A parked car or a closed room works well
  • Use headphones if others are nearby
  • Open your appointment link 5–10 minutes early to test your camera and sound
  • Charge your device or keep it plugged in
  • Have your medication list and questions handy
  • Know the address where you'll be, in case we need to send help in an emergency
  • Keep your phone nearby. If the video drops, your clinician will call you
Your privacy

Confidentiality, explained simply

What you share with us stays private. We won't share your information without your written permission, with a few exceptions required by law to keep people safe.

Safety

If we believe you or someone else is in serious, immediate danger, we may need to take steps to keep everyone safe. That could mean contacting emergency services or someone at risk.

Abuse or neglect

Clinicians are required by law to report suspected abuse or neglect of a child, an older adult or a dependent adult to the proper authorities.

Court orders

In rare cases a judge can order records to be released. If that happens, we'll share only what's required and tell you whenever we're allowed to.

Your clinician will go over confidentiality with you at your first visit, and you can ask about it any time. With your permission, we may also share limited information with your insurance company (for billing) or other providers you choose. Read our full Notice of Privacy Practices.

Minors & consent

Care for teens and children

For patients under 18, a parent or legal guardian usually needs to give consent and attend part of the first appointment. In some states, teens of a certain age can consent to some mental health care on their own. We'll explain the rules that apply to your family.

Teens do best when they can trust their therapist. Parents are kept informed about overall progress, attendance and any safety concerns, while the details of what a teen shares in session usually stay private. We talk through this with everyone at the start so expectations are clear.

  • A parent or guardian signs consent forms for most minors
  • If parents share custody, we may ask for custody paperwork
  • Teens get age-appropriate privacy, and safety always comes first
  • Family sessions are available when helpful
  • Learn more about care for teens & young adults
Your rights

Your rights as a patient

You're a partner in your care. As our patient, you have the right to:

  • Be treated with dignity and respect, free from discrimination
  • Understand your diagnosis, treatment options and their risks and benefits
  • Take part in decisions about your care, and say no to any treatment
  • Ask for a different clinician at any time
  • Privacy and confidentiality, within the limits of the law
  • See and request a copy of your records
  • Clear information about fees and billing before services begin
  • Language interpretation and accessibility accommodations at no cost
  • Voice a concern or file a complaint without it affecting your care
  • End treatment whenever you choose

Questions or concerns about your rights? Call (555) 010-0123 and ask for our patient advocate, or see our non-discrimination notice.

FAQ

Common questions from new patients

How long does therapy usually take?

It depends on your goals. Some people feel better after 8–12 sessions focused on a specific concern; others prefer longer-term support. Most people start with weekly sessions and meet less often as things improve. You and your clinician will check in regularly about what's working and when you're ready to step down or finish.

How will I know if it's working?

We'll set clear goals together and check progress along the way, sometimes with short questionnaires. Signs of progress might include sleeping better, feeling less overwhelmed, or handling hard moments more easily. Change isn't always a straight line, and it's always OK to tell your clinician if something isn't helping.

Can I switch clinicians if it's not a good fit?

Yes, absolutely. A good fit matters a lot, and switching is common and never held against you. Just let your clinician or our care coordinators know, and we'll help you find someone new without starting over from scratch.

Do you prescribe medication?

Yes. Our psychiatric providers offer evaluations and ongoing medication management, and they coordinate with your therapist. Therapists themselves don't prescribe, but they can refer you for an evaluation if medication might help. Medication is always your choice. See psychiatry & medication.

How long is the wait for a first appointment?

Most new patients are seen within 1–2 weeks. Wait times can vary by clinician, specialty and whether you need evenings or weekends. If you need to be seen sooner, tell us when you call and we'll do our best, including adding you to our cancellation list.

Do I need a referral?

Usually not. You can contact us directly. Some insurance plans (often HMOs) do require a referral from your primary care doctor, so it's worth checking with your plan. We're happy to help you find out. Learn more on our Insurance & Fees page.

What if I need to cancel or miss an appointment?

Life happens. Please give us at least 24 hours' notice by phone or the patient portal so we can offer the time to someone else. Late cancellations or missed appointments may be charged a fee, which insurance doesn't cover. If something urgent comes up, call us and we'll work with you.

Will my employer find out I'm in treatment?

No. We don't share anything with your employer without your written permission. If you use insurance through work, your employer does not see your health records or what you discuss in sessions. If you're using an Employee Assistance Program (EAP), it's confidential too; we'd only confirm attendance if you ask us to.

Is telehealth as effective as in-person care?

For most concerns, research shows video therapy works about as well as in-person sessions. Some people prefer to mix both. If telehealth isn't the right fit, for example if you don't have a private space, we'll talk through other options.

What if I can't afford care or don't have insurance?

We offer self-pay rates and a limited number of sliding-scale spots, and we can provide a Good Faith Estimate of costs before your first visit. Please ask. We'll help you explore options. See Insurance & Fees.

What should I do if I'm in crisis before my first appointment?

Please don't wait. Call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) any time, call 911, or go to the nearest emergency room. More options are on our Crisis Resources page.
